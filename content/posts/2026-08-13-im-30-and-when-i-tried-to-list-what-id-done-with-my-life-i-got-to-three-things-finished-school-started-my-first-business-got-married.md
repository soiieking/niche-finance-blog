There's a thread on r/sideproject that's been rattling around my head for a week. A user, 30 years old, sits down to inventory their life and comes up with three items: finished school, started a business, got married.

That's it.

No "scaled to $10k MRR." No "acquired by a unicorn." No "ran a marathon in under four hours." Just three honest, heavy things.

The comments are where it gets interesting. One user, u/throwaway_dev_42, responds with something like: "I'm 34. I've launched 11 side projects. Nine failed. One makes $200/month. The other got acquired for less than my annual salary. I still show up to my day job." That comment has hundreds of upvotes.

Here's my take: the thread isn't about failure. It's about the lie we've all bought into.

## The Scoreboard Is Rigged

We've internalized a metric system that doesn't measure a life. It measures a highlight reel. The indie hacker community is uniquely guilty of this. We scroll past "I quit my job to build X" posts and feel a pang of inadequacy, ignoring that the poster's parents funded their rent for two years.

The r/sideproject thread cuts through that noise. The OP isn't asking for advice. They're asking for permission to feel okay. And the community, to its credit, mostly delivered.

One commenter pointed out something I haven't stopped thinking about: "Your business is a thing you do. Your marriage is a thing you build. School is a thing you survive. The first two are ongoing. The last one is done. That's not a short list. That's a full plate."

## The "Three Things" Are Actually Heavy

Let's be honest about what's on that list.

**Finished school.** In 2026, that means you navigated a system that's actively hostile to critical thinking, probably took on debt, and didn't quit. That's not nothing. That's a baseline of discipline most people don't have.

**Started a business.** This is the one the thread gets weird about. The OP doesn't say it's profitable. Doesn't say it's scaled. Just says it exists. And honestly? That's the hardest part. I've built and broken enough side projects to know that the gap between "I have an idea" and "I have a registered entity with a bank account" is where 90% of people die.

**Got married.** This is the one that makes the hustle bros uncomfortable. It's not a metric. It's not a milestone you can A/B test. It's a daily, unglamorous commitment that doesn't show up on any dashboard.

## What the Thread Gets Wrong

Not everyone in the comments is helpful. There's always a guy who shows up to say "You should have 10x'd your business by now" or "Marriage is a distraction from your goals." That's the toxic underbelly of the community, and it deserves to be called out.

One commenter, u/quiet_builder, nailed the counter: "I've been married 12 years. My business failed twice. My wife is the reason I tried a third time. The business is now my full-time job. The marriage is the infrastructure. The business is the product."

That's the real insight. The three things aren't a list of achievements. They're a stack. School is the foundation. Marriage is the infrastructure. The business is the application layer. You don't rank them. You run them.

## The Practical Takeaway

If you're 30 and feeling behind, here's the blunt truth: you're not. The median age for a successful first-time founder is somewhere in the mid-30s. The "I'm 22 and exited" stories are survivorship bias on steroids. They're also usually funded by someone's trust fund.

What actually matters, based on years of watching this community:

- **Your business doesn't need to be big. It needs to be alive.** A $500/month side project that runs itself is more valuable than a $50k/month project that requires 80-hour weeks.
- **Your marriage (or relationship) is a compounding asset.** It's not a distraction. It's the thing that keeps you sane when the product launch flops.
- **School is done. Stop carrying it.** The debt might linger, but the identity shouldn't.

## The Bottom Line

The OP's three-item list isn't a confession of failure. It's a statement of priorities. And in a community obsessed with growth metrics, that's the most contrarian thing you can say.

I haven't tested this theory on everyone, and the community is genuinely split on whether marriage helps or hurts a founder's trajectory. Your mileage may vary. But I'll take the guy with three solid things over the guy with fifty half-finished projects and a divorce settlement any day.

The scoreboard is rigged. Stop playing that game. Build your stack.
