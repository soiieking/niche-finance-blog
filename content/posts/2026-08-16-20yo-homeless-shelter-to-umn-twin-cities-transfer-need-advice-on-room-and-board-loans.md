I've been lurking in the r/personalfinance thread where a 20-year-old is seeking advice on room and board loans after transferring to UMN Twin Cities from a homeless shelter. First off, kudos to this person for turning their life around - that's a hell of a story. Now, about those loans: the commenter "frugalfutura" brings up a solid point that federal student loans should be the first port of call, given their generally lower interest rates (around 4.5% for undergrads) and more forgiving repayment terms.

This is overkill for most people, but I love the suggestion to use a loan calculator like the one on NerdWallet or Student Loan Hero to get a sense of the total cost of borrowing. For instance, if our transfer student needs $10,000 for room and board, they're looking at around $100-150 per month for 10 years, assuming that 4.5% interest rate. Not bad, but it adds up.

One commenter, "sensible_saver22", mentions that they wish they'd explored more scholarship options before taking on debt. I'm with them on this - there are tons of scholarships out there, even for transfer students. The UMN website lists a bunch, and sites like Fastweb or Scholarships.com are worth checking out. It's not a guarantee, buthey, $1,000 here or there can make a big difference in the grand scheme.

The community is genuinely split on whether it's better to take on a part-time job or focus on studies. I lean towards the former - having some income can help offset living expenses and reduce the need for loans in the first place. That being said, our transfer student should prioritize their studies, especially if they're on a scholarship or have a tight GPA requirement to maintain. Maybe aim for 10-15 hours of work per week, depending on the course load.

Someone in the thread mentions that they're considering a private loan with a 6% interest rate. I'd advise against that unless absolutely necessary - federal loans are generally a better bet, and 6% is pretty steep. If you do need to go the private route, SoFi or Discover might be worth looking into, but be sure to read the fine print. This is not a decision to take lightly, and it's crucial to understand the terms before signing on the dotted line.

I haven't dug into the specifics of UMN's financial aid office, but it's worth our transfer student's time to get in touch with them and discuss their options. They may be able to offer some guidance on loan forgiveness programs or other forms of assistance. As "collegefinanceguru" points out, it's all about being proactive and exploring every avenue.

### Next Steps
Our transfer student has a few options on the table, from federal loans to part-time work. The key is to weigh these options carefully and consider the long-term implications. With some careful planning and perseverance, they can minimize their debt and set themselves up for success.

For now, I'd recommend our transfer student stick with federal loans and aim to borrow as little as possible. It's not the most glamorous approach, but it's a solid foundation to build on. And hey, if they can scrounge up some extra cash through a part-time job or scholarship, that's just gravy.

### FAQs
If you're in a similar situation, here are a few questions you might have:
* Q: What's the difference between federal and private loans?
A: Federal loans generally have lower interest rates and more forgiving repayment terms. 
* Q: How do I apply for scholarships as a transfer student?
A: Check the UMN website, as well as sites like Fastweb or Scholarships.com. 
* Q: Can I work part-time and still receive financial aid?
A: Yes, but be sure to check with the UMN financial aid office to understand how your income might impact your aid package.
