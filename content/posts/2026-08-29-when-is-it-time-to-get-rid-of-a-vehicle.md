Not sure if you should keep fixing your car or bite the bullet and buy a newer one? It's a classic dilemma: financial prudence vs. practical reality. The crew over at [r/personalfinance](https://www.reddit.com/r/personalfinance/) has been hashing this out for years. Their collective wisdom boils down to a handful of common-sense principles. If you're feeling stuck, here’s a curated roundup of their best arguments.  
## The Cost-to-Keep Test: When Repairs Outweigh the Value  
User **u/FutureChoice123** put it bluntly: "If a repair costs more than the car’s total value, it’s time to walk away." Seems simple, but let’s add some nuance. Are we talking about a single catastrophic repair (like a blown engine)? Or are you pouring small amounts into a never-ending cascade of maintenance?   
A few people pointed out real numbers here. For instance, if your old sedan is worth $2,000, but the transmission is going to cost $2,500 to fix, that’s a non-starter for most folks. But if it’s a $500 fix this time (and nothing else major is looming), you might want to ride it out.  
**Big exception**: rare or hobby cars. If you’re driving something like a ‘90s Toyota Land Cruiser or an E30 BMW, value isn’t just about the blue book. Those are worth fixing—even if repairs tip slightly above value—since their resale and collector interest can bounce back. For your basic 2008 Camry? Not so much.  
## How Often Are You Fixing It?  
Several threads leaned on this idea: Track your repair costs over a year. Rule of thumb? If you’re spending more than 10-15% of the cost of a newer (but not new) replacement car annually, it’s upgrade time.  
**u/PracticalDough94** shared their example: “I kept my car past 200k miles, but once I hit $2,500 in repairs that were only getting me through the year, I traded it in.”  
Let’s do some quick math here. If you’d spend $2,500 per year to keep a $2,000 car limping along, it’s smarter to upgrade to a reliable $12,000 used car ASAP. The new car’s warranty and lower annual maintenance alone could save you hundreds annually.  
### Know Your Tolerance for Chaos  
Older cars come with baggage: random dashboard lights, surprise breakdowns, and an “is it gonna start today?” roulette.  
This was divisive in the thread. **u/HondaForever789** said, “I’d rather keep my $1,000 Civic, even if it took $1,500 in repairs last year. That’s still cheaper than the car payment.” Great if you have a cheap commuter car and tolerate some hassle.  
But **u/OverIt2024** chimed in with the opposite stance: “Old cars wreck your schedule. If you need dependable transport for work, a newer car is worth the premium.”  
This is where self-honesty matters. If missing work for a radiator failure costs you more than a $300 repair, maybe it’s time to trade up.  
## Gas Mileage and Safety: Sneakier Factors  
Cars that are 15+ years old weren’t designed with today’s fuel efficiency—or safety tech. If gas is $4 per gallon and your clunker gets 20 MPG, upgrading to something like a used Prius (50 MPG) saves you over $1,000 a year on fuel alone if you drive 15,000 miles annually.  
Then there’s the elephant in the room: safety. **u/WhyDriveOld** nailed it: “Could you survive a crash in your current car?” Older vehicles lack advanced airbags, crumple zones, and ABS. If you’ve got a family or drive in hazardous conditions, this isn’t the place to save money.  
## To Loan or Not to Loan?  
Everyone on r/personalfinance freaks out about car loans, and for a good reason: high interest rates are trashing budgets. But if you’re looking at $5,000 in repairs vs. taking on a $15,000 car loan at 8%, you might be better off with the loan if it keeps your transportation reliable.  
The key? Minimize the loan term (three years or less) and don’t buy more car than you need. As **u/MoneyMoose44** said: “Some people replace a beater with a $40k SUV. That’s not an upgrade; it’s a flex. Be smarter than that.”  
## Final Thoughts  
What’s the Reddit consensus? If the car still runs reliably without constant repairs, keep it. If you’re facing major repairs that threaten your budget—or can’t stomach another breakdown at rush hour—sell it and upgrade to something affordable and dependable.  
Every situation is a little different, so keep the receipts, know your numbers, and trust your gut.  
### FAQ  
#### How long should I keep a car?  
There’s no universal answer, but Reddit suggests keeping it as long as repairs stay below 10-15% of a reliable used car’s cost annually. Once you’re chasing expensive repairs, it’s time to upgrade.  
#### Should I buy a brand-new car or a used one?  
Used is the better deal for most people, especially in the 3-5 year range. You dodge the steepest depreciation while getting something reliable. But if you’re sitting on a great credit score and plan to keep the car for 10+ years, new could work.  
#### What’s the best age or mileage for a used car?  
Most r/personalfinance users agree: Sweet spot is 3-5 years old with under 60,000 miles. You avoid new-car price tags but get lots of driving life ahead.
