Ten days ago, I posted on r/sideproject that I had an app nobody wanted. Zero users. Dead air. A few kind strangers tore it apart (gently) and told me what my big, dumb mistakes were. I listened, fixed a handful of things, and today? Six people have paid for it.

Six customers isn’t exactly retirement money, right? But this isn’t about the cash. It’s that someone saw value in something I made. That’s wild. Let me tell you what changed.

## My First Attempt: The Over-Engineered Dumpster Fire

I’ll be honest: my original app wasn’t bad in a fundamental sense. It was functional. It solved a problem. But the execution was a mess. Imagine a Swiss Army knife that weighs 10 pounds and also tries to make coffee. Nobody wants that.

One r/sideproject commenter nailed it. They said, “Your app feels like it was made for *you* but not for anyone else.” Ouch, but true. I’d crammed the thing full of features that I thought were cool but didn’t matter to normal people — like the ability to customize everything through 18 different toggles. Overkill.

The other big mistake? Charging $19/month for a product that, frankly, screamed “weekend project.” That pricing made users think the app was meant to be life-changing, but the experience wasn’t polished enough to back that up. I’d set expectations I couldn’t deliver on. Classic rookie move.

## The Feedback That Actually Helped

Here’s what I got out of the Reddit feedback:

1. **Kill the complexity.** The most common advice was “Cut out 80% of the bloat and double down on the one thing your app does well.” So I did. Instead of trying to be everything, the app now does *one thing*: it lets users schedule recurring weekly reminders — without having to sign up. Dead simple.

2. **Rethink pricing.** Someone pointed out that $19/month is what you charge for a pro tool, not a scrappy utility. They recommended trying $4.99 as a one-time fee. I wasn’t sure (felt weird to drop my ego about recurring revenue), but they were absolutely right.

3. **Fix the onboarding flow.** Oh man, my original onboarding process was like navigating tax paperwork. Four screens before you even got to try the app. I cut that down to a single screen. As soon as the app opens, you can start setting reminders immediately. No account creation. No BS.

## The Result: Six People Paid

Here’s the crazy part. I launched the updated version (v1.2.1, for reference) just four days ago. Since then, there’ve been 140 downloads and six one-time sales. I know, these are tiny numbers. But my conversion rate is around 4%, which feels *decent* for a $4.99 utility app.

Reddit wasn’t just helpful for fixing the app — they also helped me stay sane. Someone reminded me: “You only need 100 super-fans to make $500/month. You don’t need everyone.” That totally reframed how I thought about success. I don’t need the app to go viral. I just need a small group of people who genuinely find it useful.

Also, real talk — those six sales were deeply validating. You only see the $4.99, but I see everything behind it: the nights spent coding after work, debugging while eating cold pizza, questioning whether the project was totally pointless. So yeah, when someone chooses to open their wallet? It’s a big deal.

## What’s Next?

I know this is just the beginning. Six sales don’t mean I’ve “made it.” But now I have a clear roadmap for what’s worth improving — and what isn’t.

Next steps:
- **Collect more feedback.** I added a simple “What could be better?” popup in the app. So far, two people have responded, and their ideas are gold.
- **Push a tiny update every 2 weeks.** Even if it’s small — an icon change, a bug fix — just to keep momentum.
- **Experiment with distribution.** Someone in the thread suggested posting on Product Hunt. I was waiting until I felt worthy (dumb reason), but I’m going for it now.

Your app doesn’t need to change the world. It just needs to do one thing well for a small group of people. Fixing my app wasn’t about adding more — it was about cutting the fluff and listening hard. Turns out, people will pay for simple, useful things. Who knew?
